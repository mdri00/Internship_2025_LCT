\tabto{1 cm}The easiest way to account for the transition between stages is to write that the rate of change of the stage population $x$ is proportional to the stage population.  In other words, $\frac{dx}{dt} = -mx$, m is the maturation rate of the stage and 1/m is the mean maturation time. With this approach, models belong to the Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) class. This approach allows parametrizing the timing of transition but it does not allow to control the variability of maturation time inside the stage population. The ODE framework implicitly assumes an underlying stochastic state transmission model with an exponentially distributed dwell time in the stage [Hurtado]. Particularly, it assumes that the probability of transition is maximal at the entrance of the stage. Robertson and co-workers (2018) showed that about half of the stage-structured models found in recent literature use the ODE framework to account for the transition between states. In line with the work of Gurney and co-workers in 1983, most remaining models reviewed by Robertson et al. (2018) majorly used fixed delays through Delayed Differential Equations (DDE). These models assume that all the individuals of a given cohort mature synchronically, i.e. that the maturation delays are fixed. This means that the models do not account for within-species (within-stage) variability in maturation time.  Overall, despite their flexibility on the parametrization of mean maturation time, the two approaches (ODE and DDE)  rely on fundamentally different assumptions on maturation time variability. Hereafter, maturation variability will be understood as the the coefficient of variation (CV) of the maturation time, i.e. its standard deviation divided by its mean. We prefer this definition over the variance because, contrary to the variance, a greater mean development time does not entail a greater CV. 

\tabto{1cm} Besides, maturation variability in the CV sense can significantly influence the models' predictions—broadly understood here as any qualitative or quantitative property displayed by the model. While, [in population dynamics], typical analyses focus on equilibrium densities and stability, we also consider properties of oscillations, such as their amplitude and period. This broader interpretation allows us to capture a wider spectrum of dynamical behaviors relevant to both theoretical understanding and applied ecological management. In the same spirit, we will examine how maturation variability interacts with other demographic parameters that could be influenced by agroecological levers beyond parasitoid introduction. This includes, for example, factors affected by practices like insect confusion, nets, natural predator(s) introduction, or the sterile insect technique (SIT) (Biocontrole citation?). Our approach also follows the line of work by Wearing et al. (2004) and Nakamichi et al. (2008), who highlighted how maturation variability influences not just stability but also the persistence of a host-parasitoid model with egg cannibalism and larval competition. 

\tabto{1 cm} The use of fixed delays was shown to be a destabilizing factor of community dynamics as early as the 1930s (Bailey 1931, cited in Smith and Mead 1974). Seeking higher biological realism, developmental time variability was subsequently introduced and shown to be an important stabilizing factor of community dynamics, including pest populations (Smith and Mead 1974, Blythe et al 1984, Bellows 1986, Briggs 1993, Abrams and Quince 2005, Xu 2010, de Valpine 2014, Robertson 2018), and notably so with experimental support (Cronin et al 2016).

A clear review of the methods used to assess developmental variability was given by de Valpine and co-workers (2014). Empirical distributions of maturation time often display a delay where no maturation occurs, a clear mode as well as a relatively pronounced right-skewness. \tabto{1 cm} The model of Murdoch et al. (1987) constitutes one of the simplest host-parasitoid models that introduces a stage-structure and that shows the direct effect of the added structure. \tabto{1 cm} The concept of variability has been very important in explaining the stability of laboratory and natural host-parasitoid (and predator-prey) systems (Hassel 2001). Risk heterogeneity is defined by the variability (in the CV sense) of parasitism risk among hosts (Hassel 2001). Generally, it was shown to stabilize populations (Chesson and Murdoch 1984, Hassel 2001ab, Taylor 1993). Partly because it was more easily observable in natural systems (through the measure of the density dependence of parasitism, Hassel 2001), risk heterogeneity was originally mainly understood as a spatial variability (Taylor 1993). The influential work of May (1978) introduces a relatively simple phenomenological incorporation of spatial patchiness into a host-parasitoid model with non-overlapping generations (Hassel 2001, Taylor 1993). The clumping, or overdispersion, term k is introduced and equal to $1/CV_p^2$, $CV_p$ being the coefficient of variation of the among-patch distribution of parasitoids. In other words, one new parameter, k, is introduced to account for cases were parasitism is not homogeneously distributed among hosts: parasitism risk heterogeneity can be added (and quantified) by picking a non-infinite value of this new parameter k. Since (a non-infinite) k makes the parasitism rate saturate with the density of parasitoids, the effect of this parameter mimics the effect of a form of interference between parasitoids (May 1978). This was thus named pseudo-interference. This form of density-dependence, regardless of how it is implemented, constitute the basis for the stabilizing effect of risk heterogeneity. It makes per capita parasitoid recruitment more density dependent, and also makes host mortality less dependent on parasitoid density, thereby dampening the oscillations in parasitoid density (Taylor 1993). The pseudo-interference term introduced by May (1978) has been derived in a continuous form (i.e. transformed into a rate term) in ulterior overlapping generation models (Godfray and Hassel 1989, other?). 

\tabto{1 cm} Xu et al. (2010) brilliantly [trouver un mot moins fort] remarked that developmental variability could be understood as another form of risk heterogeneity. Thus, on one side, May's k parameter received a considerable attention and contributed to the comprehension of the empirical, yet unexplained, stability of host-parasitoid systems (Hassel 2001). On the other side, developmental variability has been considered for a long time but never really gained the central position occupied by spatial forms of risk heterogeneity (Taylor 1993). Though, the number of LCT subcompartment n might offer the same advantages as May's clumping parameter k, namely realism coupled to modeling simplicity. In the third and last part of this study, we will thus try to answer the following : can we find an equivalence between the effect of k and the effect of n ? Knowing they both introduce a form of risk heterogeneity, do they quantitatively have the same effect of the system?

\subsection{Murdoch (1987) model}
\tabto{1 cm} We consider the continuous-time host-parasitoid model detailed in Murdoch et al. 1987 in a DDE approach. [something about the fact that this model is said "strategic" et une def?]. The host and parasitoid life cycles are divided into 2 stages: juvenile ($H_1, P_1$) and reproductive adult ($H_2, P_2$). Parasitism occurs on the juvenile host stage $H_1$, and parasitoids search randomly and independently at rate a such that the rate of $H_1$ mortality from parasitism is $aP_2(t)H_1(t)$. Adults die through a constant mortality rate, which means that the adult survival time follows an exponential distribution. There is no density-dependent component to host mortality. Is can be justified by the fact that host populations are much below the level where food or other resources can be limiting if the (bio)control is successful (Murdoch 1987). From now on, except in Figure 2c, the temporal scale is normalized by the maturation time of the host: $m_H = 1/T_{H1} = 1$. The application of the LCT to obtain a distributed-delay model as close as possible as the fixed-delay model introduced by Murdoch is detailed in Appendix A1.

The resulting LCT-ODE system of equation is given by equation (1) (or (X) in Appendix), with notation $\dot{X} := \frac{dX}{dt}$. \\

\tabto{1 cm} In all models, the mean development times are set equal, and the proportion of juvenile survival can be equalized too (future publication of J.Guilberteau). However, for simplicity, the parametrization was kept simple. Ignoring juvenile intrinsic mortality,  the constraint on the mean development time gives (in the case of the host, the parasitoid has an analogous formula) $\theta_H = n*m_H = n/T_{H1}$. With this parametrization, the proportion of juvenile survival depends on n (see Appendix and Discussion). Apart from this, the construction of the LCT-ODE model is made such that the only difference between all the models are maturation time variability. 

\begin{equation}
\begin{dcases}
\dot{H_{1,1}}(t) = H_{1,1}(t)(-a P_2(t) - \delta_{H1} - \theta_H) + r H_2(t) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{H_{1,i}}(t) = \theta_H(H_{1,i-1}(t) - H_{1,i}(t)) - \delta_{H1} H_{1,i}(t) - aP_2H_{1,i}(t), \quad i \in (2, 3, \dots, n) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{H_{2}}(t) = \theta_H H_{1,n}(t) - \delta_{H2} H_{2}(t) \\
\dot{P_{1,1}}(t) = P_{1,1}(t)(- \delta_{P1} - \theta_P) + a P_2(t) \sum_{i=1}^{n} H_{1,i}(t) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{P_{1,i}}(t) = \theta_P(P_{1,i-1}(t) - P_{1,i}(t)) - \delta_{P1} P_{1,i}(t), \quad i \in (2, 3, \dots, m) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{P_{2}}(t) = \theta_P P_{1,m}(t) - \delta_{P2} P_{2}(t) \\
\end{dcases}
\end{equation}

\tabto{1 cm} The local stability of the non-trivial equilibria of the system will be analyzed using linear stability theory. Specifically, the characteristic equation derived from the linearized system at each equilibrium point yields a set of roots. These roots correspond to the eigenvalues of the jacobian matrix in the case of a ODE-type system. The real parts of these roots determine the local behavior of the system near the equilibrium. If all the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix evaluated at a non-trivial equilibrium have strictly negative real parts, then the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable. Conversely, if at least one eigenvalue has a positive real part, the equilibrium is unstable. 
\tabto{1 cm} Local stability diagrams will be generated. They map out regions of parameter space where equilibria are stable or unstable. These diagrams are built by systematically computing the Jacobian matrix across a grid of parameter values and analyzing the sign of the real parts of its eigenvalues. When an eigenvalue's real part crosses 0, a boundary is drawn. Here, the two parameter axes will be the longevity of the adult host $T_{H2} := 1/\delta_{H2}$ and the maturation time of the parasitoid $T_{P1} := 1/m_P$ which were found to have a strong impact on stability. Since $T_{H1}$ is set to 1 and defines the time scale, we will either write $T_{P1}/T_{H1}$ or simply $T_{P1}$ (same for $T_{H2}$). The diagrams were generated using the function Eigenvalues of Wolfram Mathematica (Version 14.1) applied to the Jacobian matrix of the LCT-ODE system. 
\tabto{1 cm} Bifurcation diagram will also be generated. These diagrams show equilibria or periodic solutions as functions of a  parameter, highlighting points where stability changes (i.e. bifurcations). By giving information about the periodic solutions when the asymptotic behavior of the model are limit cycles, bifurcation diagram are complementary to local stability diagram. To evaluate these oscillations, numerical simulations were realized using the Mathematica function NDSolve with an automatic (and dynamically changing) solver method. In the light of the frequent stiffness issues of the simulations, NDSolve was found to offer the most fast and reliable solver compared to other tested methods with various Python libraries. For further details, relevant Mathematica and Python scripts can be found on [GITHUB?].



\subsection{Godfray and Hassel (1989) model}
\tabto{1 cm} Godfray and Hassel (1989) introduced a slightly complexified version of the Murdoch 1987 DDE model. First, two invulnerable juvenile stages ($H_1$ and $H_3$) were added for the host and surround the vulnerable stage $H_2$. Second, the parasitism term was changed to involve density-dependence to $P_2$. Parasitism thus saturates with the (local) density of adult parasitoids : this is [was?] called pseudo-interference. The ODE-LCT models construction is detailed in [Appendix 2]. Details about the system can be found in the appendix. \subsection{Comparing the effect of May's implementation of parasitism heterogeneity and the effect of developmental variability}
\tabto{1 cm} To compare the stabilizing effect of May's parasitism clumping parameter k and of developmental variability entailed by the number of LCT subcompartments n, the simplest model with 2 stages for both host and parasitoid was chosen. One form of risk heterogeneity was implemented at a time. A model with a homogeneous search by the parasitoids (k $\to \infty$ so the rate of parasitism is $a*P2(t)$) coupled with developmental variability (n finite) was thus compared to a model with a heterogeneous search by the parasitoids (k finite) coupled with fixed delays (n $\to \infty$). For simplicity, the latter model was implemented and analyzed with a DDE system of equations, similarly to what was done in Murdoch (1987). The only difference with their model is the introduction of a non-linear term for the rate of parasitism, namely $k*ln(1 + \frac{a P2(t)}{k})$ (Godfray and Hassel 1989).

\section{Discussion (d'abord sous forme de bullet points}
\subsection{Differential sensitivity of models to developmental variability}


\tabto{1.5 cm}  In line with other studies of host-parasitoid/prey-predator systems (Smith and Mead 1974, Hastings 1984, Wearing 2004, Abrams and Quince 2005, de Valpine 2014), and a precedent study directly conducted on the same systems (Xu et al. 2010), developmental variability (DV) substantially stabilizes both host-parasitoid systems introduced by Murdoch et al. (1987) and Godfray and Hassel (1989).  It was unclear, however, how sensitive these systems are to DV [C'est pas exactement ça en vrai : on peut dire que la sensibilité c'est la diff entre ODE et DDE, puis on regarde quantitativement le chgmt des propriétés le long de l'axe ? Mais j'aime bien le concept de differential sensitivity inspiré de Wearing donc j'ai pas trop envie de toucher à cette phrase]. In other words, knowing that the ODE-exponentially-distributed-type (n=1) system generally has very distinct properties from its DDE-type counterpart, does the behavior of the system change swiftly as n increase from 1, does it change very progressively between n=1 and $n \to \infty$, or does the system display some properties only when n is very large, i.e. when the delay is almost perfectly fixed? Here, using the Linear Chain Trick, we showed that the equilibrium densities and the oscillations properties of both system were rather the first type. However, the stability properties of the systems are differentially sensitive to DV. 
\tabto{1.5 cm} Murdoch et al. (1987) introduced a two-stage host-parasitoid system with an invulnerable adult host which longevity stabilizes the system through a refuge effect. DV causes a strong stabilizing effect, especially at low n (range 1-30). Sensitivity to developmental variability is thus of the first type again. This may result from an 'early bird' effect. Low-shape Erlang distributions (notably at n = 1) allow a broad, early window for maturation, with a high probability near t=0 (Figure 2). A high DV enables more juveniles to mature quickly, reducing their exposure to parasitism. DV thus complements adult longevity by increasing the number of individuals in the refuge, which stabilizes population dynamics. However, the dependency of the effect of host fecundity on stability on DV is more surprising. Host fecundity is destabilizing in case of a fixed delay. This is rather expected considering that fixed-delay systems approach the behavior of discrete-time systems, the latter also showing a negative relation between host fecundity and stability. Conversely, host fecundity is destabilizing when DV is very high, here at n = 1 or 2 (CV > 0.7). We may not provide a secure interpretation of these contrasting effects, but we point out their importance in the context of biocontrol. Other levers of biocontrol, applied in combination with the use of auxiliary species like parasitoids, may affect host fecundity (for instance through confusion or the sterile-male technique). It is thus unclear what effect on population dynamics the combination of these levers could have.


\tabto{1.5 cm} Godfray and Hassel (1989) collected examples of cycles in tropical insects where the cycle period is roughly equal to the development time of the host, and no climatic fluctuation could account for the oscillating dynamics (Godray and Hassel 1989). They proved that a phenological mismatch can cause single-generation cycles ("GC") in populations (Godfray and Hassel 1989, Godfray et al.1994). This happens if the ratio between host and parasitoid development times (ratioT) leads to a gap between host and parasitoid population peaks. This will lead to high parasitism at times when the host population is at a low point, further reinforcing the trough (Murdoch 2003).  To reproduce this behavior in a "strategic" model, they developed a DDE model that indeed predicts GCs for generation time ratios equal to 0.5, 1.5, ... Xu et al. (2010) showed that a high developmental variability, here introduced through shifted-Gamma distributions with shapes 3,4 and 11, suppresses the "phenological mismatch" effect (i.e. the occurrence of GC oscillations) when ratioT is superior to 1. However, it was unclear how the system behaves when the Erlang shape is superior: around 50, 100 or even 300. How small does DV has to be in order to recover the phenological mismatch effect at high ratioT ? Besides, the effect of DV on what could be qualified as the "waiting" $H_1$ and $H_3$ stages (at least non-interacting) was not explored. We showed that DV must be very low ($n_2$ and m at least superior 50 to 75, i.e. CV < 0.15) for the system to display GCs at a ratio between parasitoid and host generation time (ratioT) > 1. Even for the the case ratioT $\approx$ 0.5 which is more robust to DV on $H_2$ and $P_1$, DV added on the remaining stages $H_1$ and $H_3$ can stabilize the system enough to curb GC oscillations. Overall, in this model, stability induced by DV tends to dominate the unstable behavior of single-generation oscillations. This phenological mismatch effect is thus not very robust to more realistic hypothesis about stage transition.


\tabto{1.5 cm}A simple time-delay model with cohort competition was built by Gurney et al. (1980, 1983) to explain seemingly discrete generations in \textit{Lucilia cuprina} laboratory population dynamics. In case of a fixed delay, the non-trivial equilibrium displays a "stability switch", which means that as the delay grows, it goes from stable to unstable to stable again. It was shown, using a Gamma-distributed maturation time that there exists a maximal maturation time variance beyond which no stability switch occurs (Beretta 2016). This means that DV restrains [meilleur mot?] the unstable windows. From another point of view, this means that there exists a minimal number of LCT subcompartment n such that an unstability window suddenly appears in the corresponding stability diagram. For the parameters chosen by Gurney (1980), we found (work not shown in this study) that this windows appears between n=8 and n=9. This would thus be another case of a sharp, non linear change in the model's behavior as DV changes, as well as a maximal sensitivity to DV at high DV.  Beretta et al. (2016) showed that the existence of a DV threshold that suppresses the stability switch depends on the balance between the insect's fecundity and adult mortality rates on one side, and juvenile mortality rate on the other. If the latter is sufficiently high, then the equilibria are asymptotically stable independently of the delay variance. This underlines that the effect of DV on dynamics is dependent on the other demographic parameters. Wearing et al. (2004) studied a host-parasitoid model with larval competition and cannibalism. They showed that DV can provoke a qualitative change in the predicted dynamics, namely the transition from single-generation cycles to multigeneration cycles. They however emphasized that the effect of DV on population dynamics was dependent on several factors: 1. on which stage DV is added ; 2. on which dynamic displayed by the system is studied ; 3. on the used set of parameters and their distance to a pre-existing bifurcation point (for example: if adult longevity is set very high in the Murdoch system, the increase of DV will have low to no effect as the system is already stable ; or the work of Beretta et al. 2016). Our work is very much in line with these results: Godfray and Hassel's model illustrate 1. and 2., and both model (see SFig bifurc with not effect of n) illustrate 3. Yet, we haven't fully explored the interactions between developmental variability and other demographic parameters in more complex models like the one of Godfray and Hassel (1989). This could be the object of an ulterior work.

\tabto{1.5 cm} It is possible to apply the LCT technique to adults, introducing a potentially more realistic assumption about death time distribution [OU : "a better control over adult death time variability"]. The use of an Erlang-distributed death time rather than an exponentially distributed one might destabilize the system (Gurney 1983), or have no effect (Xu et al. 2010). Moreover, the exponential distribution appears more realistic to model death times than maturation times. For these reasons, we decided not to pursue this direction further.

\subsection{Understanding the effect of developmental variability}

\tabto{1.5 cm} The non-trivial equilibrium densities strongly increase when developmental variability is very large (typically for $n \leq 5$). It is possible (see Appendix, J. Guilberteau: work to be published) to show that, under a Erlang-distribution assumption on maturation times the probability to survive any juvenile stage Hx verifies
\[ p_{surv} = (\frac{\theta_{Hx}}{\theta_{Hx} + \delta_{Hx}})^n, \quad \theta_{Hx} := n*m_{Hx}, \]

\subsection{Developmental variability in laboratory and in nature}

\tabto{1 cm} Models dynamics' differential sensitivity to developmental variability underlines the need to gather information about the studied system's maturation time variability in order to evaluate what framework should be used in the model to take into account stage transitions. Blythe et al. (1984) noticed that empirical maturation time distribution often displays a minimum maturation period ($\tau_1$, named "shift" below), after which all individuals mature according to a more-or-less regular distribution with mean $\tau_2$. They suggested to calculate the ratio $\tau_1/\tau_2$ and showed that the stability and oscillations properties of their model with a Erlang-shifted distribution closely resembles those of a discrete delay model when this ratio is superior to 5. However, the implementation of such a shift imposes the use of either an IDE model, allowing for an arbitrary distribution, or a mixed ODE-DDE model that couples the LCT framework to a supplementary delay equation (example in Blythe et al. (1984)). These two framework bring computational and mathematical difficulties [compared to the relative simplicity of ODE models : reformuler]. 
\tabto{1 cm} Hurtado and Kirosingh (2019) suggested a method to bypass the IDE framework and allow modelers to assume maturation (more generally, passage) time distributions from a family called the phase-type distributions. These distributions can be thought of as the family of all possible hitting time distributions for continuous-time Markov chains. In the case of a "linear chain" of ODEs, the Erlang distribution is involved. As suggested by the example SEIR model with heterogeneity among infected individuals shown by Hurtado and Cameron (2021), this new framework allows highly flexible ODE models assuming more realistic maturation time distribution and can be computationally more efficient. Even outside this framework, the LCT can be judicious. Nelson et al. (2019) used the LCT to implement hierarchical asymmetric larval competition. In the case of this modeling of development, individuals advance through the subcompartments of their stage as they grow. Nelson et al. assumed each subcompartment's per-capita mortality rate to depend only on the density of individuals that are larger or equal to the size of each individual, i.e to the density of downstream subcompartments. Hence, the competition mortality terms uniquely depend on the downstream LCT subcompartments. In any case, all these promising method constrain the use of non-shifted Erlang distributions. Specifically they do not allow to add a supplementary fixed delay, namely a "shift" in distributions. Yet, first, empirical data about developmental variability is not always fitted with Erlang laws ; second, it is often provided with such shifts. 

\tabto{1 cm} Wagner (1984) introduced a method to fit shifted-Weibull distributions to various insects' maturation time distributions measured in laboratory. They have allowed the production of a large literature about the developmental variability of numerous insects with economical interests in agriculture.  The use of the (potentially shifted) Weibull distribution is a convenient way of summarizing the developmental data faithfully and without sharing the raw data about development timings.  However, the use of (non-shifted) Erlang distributions in host-parasitoid models entails the necessity to find the corresponding Gamma (since we allow the shape parameter to be non-integer) law parameters parameters such that both distributions are as close as possible. Xu et al. (2010) introduced a way to do using a Maximum Likelihood method. They found, setting the Gamma shift equal to the Weibull shift, that the estimated Gamma shapes (n) were either relatively small (1-10) or larger (>20) in their survey.

\subsection{Murdoch 1987 system}
\subsubsection{System construction : application of the Linear Chain Trick with a non-autonomous mortality term}
Note that Hurtado et al. 2018 as well as Lou and Sun 2022 give detailed justifications about the construction of continuous stage-structured models with an arbitrary maturation distribution and their convergence to a coupled system of ODEs in the case of the Erlang distribution. However, they do not include a non-autonomous mortality term. Let us thereby justify thoroughly how the studied LCT model was built analogously to the DDE model given by Murdoch (1987).

Let us consider the general form of the models used both in the ODE and DDE framework :  
\begin{equation}
\dot{H_{1}}(t) = B(t) - M_n(t) - \delta(t, ...) H_1(t)
\end{equation}

$H_1(t)$ is the density of juvenile hosts. $B(t)$ is the recruitment term of the juveniles, ie the birth rate. $M_n(t)$ is the maturation term. In the ODE framework, it would be of the form $M_{ODE}(t)= m_H H_1(t)$. In the DDE framework, it would be of the form $M_{DDE}(t) = B(t-\tau_H)S_{DDE}(t)$ with $S_{DDE}(t) := \exp\left( -\int_{t-\tau_H}^{t} \delta(u, ...) \, du \right)$ (Gurney and Nisbet 1983). 

$\delta_H(t, ...)$ is a mortality term. \textbf{We assume that $\delta$ can depend on both the densities of host and parasitoids}. Though, for clarity, $\delta(t, ...)$ will now be written in the form $\delta(t)$.

We give to the equation the initial conditions $H_1(0) = H_1^0$ and $B(-t) = B^{-}(t)$, $\delta(-t) = \delta^{-}(t)$, for $t \geq 0$.

To account for a arbitrary residence time distribution, the maturation term can be written in an integral form :
\begin{equation}
M_n(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s) \exp\left( -\int_{t-s}^{t} \delta(u) \, du \right) ds
\end{equation}
where $\varphi_n$ is the probability density function (PDF) of a gamma distribution of rate $\theta_H$ and shape n. Let's recall that

\begin{align}
\varphi_1(0) &= \theta_H, & \varphi_1' &= -\theta_H \varphi_1 \\
\varphi_n(0) &= 0, & \varphi_n' &= \theta_H (\varphi_{n-1} - \varphi_n) \quad \text{for } n \geq 2
\end{align}

Let us write :
\[
S(s, t) := \exp\left( -\int_{t-s}^{t} \delta(u) \, du \right)
\]
which satisfies :
\begin{equation}
S(0, t) = 1, \quad \partial_s S(s,t) + \partial_t S(s,t) = -\delta(t) S(s,t)
\end{equation}

We can thus write :
\begin{equation}
M_n(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s)S(s,t) \, ds
\end{equation}

By derivation :
\[
M_n'(t) = \int_0^{+\infty} B'(t-s)\varphi_n(s)S(s,t) \, ds + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s) \partial_t S(s,t) \, ds
\]

The first term, with an integration by part in s and using $S(0,t) = 1$, gives :
\begin{align*}
\int_0^{+\infty} B'(t-s)\varphi_n(s)S(s,t) \, ds &= -[B(t-s) \varphi_n(s) S(s,t) ]_{0}^{\infty} + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\frac{d}{ds}(\varphi_n(s)S(s,t)) \, ds \\
&\quad = -0 + \varphi_n(0) B(t) + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n'(s) S(s,t) \, ds \\
&\quad + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s) \partial_s S(s,t) \, ds
\end{align*}

With $(4)$, we have :
\begin{align*}
&\int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s) \partial_s S(s,t) \, ds  + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s) \partial_t S(s,t) \, ds \\
&\quad = - \delta(t) \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n(s) S(s,t)  \, ds \\
&\quad = - \delta(t) M_n(t)
\end{align*}

we finally get :
\[
M_n'(t) = \varphi_n(0) B(t) + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)\varphi_n'(s) S(s,t) \, ds - \delta(t) M_n(t)
\]

In the case $n = 1$, i.e in the ODE framework, using $(3)$ : 
\begin{align*}
M_{1}'(t) &= M_{ODE}'(t)= -\theta_H B(t) + \int_0^{+\infty} B(t-s)(-\theta_H \varphi_1(s)) S(s,t) \, ds - \delta(t) M_1(t) \\
&= \theta_H B(t) - (\theta_H + \delta(t)) M_1(t)
\end{align*}

And for $n \geq 2$, using $(4)$  :
\[
M_n'(t) = \theta_H M_{n-1}(t) - (\theta_H + \delta(t)) M_n(t)
\]

Thus, \(M_n\) is solution of the system :
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
\dot{M}_1(t) = \theta_H B(t) - (\theta_H + \delta(t)) M_1(t) \\
\dot{M}_k(t) = \theta_H M_{k-1}(t) - (\theta_H + \delta(t)) M_k(t), \quad k \in \{2, \dots, n\}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

The initial conditions of this system  $(M_1(0), \dots, M_N(0))$ are given by $(2)$, with $t = 0$.
Now, let us write, for \(k \in \{1, \dots, N\}\), $H_{1,k}(t) := \frac{M_k(t)}{\theta_H}$. Then : 
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
\dot{H_{1,1}}(t) = B(t) - (\theta_H + \delta(t)) H_{1,1}(t) \\
\dot{H_{1,1}}(t) = \theta_H H_{1, k-1}(t) - (\theta_H + \delta(t)) H_{1,k}(t), \quad k \in \{2, \dots, n\}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

By summing all the lines, and writing $y(t) := H_{1,1}(t) + \dots + H_{1,n}(t)$, we get :
\[
\dot{y}(t) = B(t) - \theta_H H_{1,n}(t) - \delta(t) y(t) = B(t) - M_n(t) - \delta(t) y(t)
\]

Thus, $H_{1,1} + \dots + H_{1,n}$ does satisfy the initial condition $(1)$.

In particular, if $H_{1,1}(0) + \dots + H_{1,n}(0) =  H_1^0$, then $H_1(t) = y(t) = H_{1,1}(t) + \dots + H_{1,n}(t)$ and $(1)$ is equivalent to $(8)$. This is called the Linear Chain Trick.

Applying the same method on the parasitoid maturation term with Erlang rate $\theta_P$ and shape $m$, choosing a linear parasitism term of parasitism assuming a random and homogeneous search of hosts by the parasitoids (May 1978), and assuming a linear birth rate, the final LCT system can be rewritten as a system of coupled ODEs:

\begin{equation}
\begin{dcases}
\dot{H_{1,1}}(t) = H_{1,1}(t)(-a P_2(t) - \delta_{H1} - \theta_H) + r H_2(t) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{H_{1,i}}(t) = \theta_H(H_{1,i-1}(t) - H_{1,i}(t)) - \delta_{H1} H_{1,i}(t) - aP_2H_{1,i}(t), \quad i \in (2, 3, \dots, n) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{H_{2}}(t) = \theta_H H_{1,n}(t) - \delta_{H2} H_{2}(t) \\
\dot{P_{1,1}}(t) = P_{1,1}(t)(- \delta_{P1} - \theta_P) + a P_2(t) \sum_{i=1}^{n} H_{1,i}(t) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{P_{1,i}}(t) = \theta_P(P_{1,i-1}(t) - P_{1,i}(t)) - \delta_{P1} P_{1,i}(t), \quad i \in (2, 3, \dots, m) \\
\vdots \\
\dot{P_{2}}(t) = \theta_P P_{1,m}(t) - \delta_{P2} P_{2}(t) \\
\end{dcases}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Equilibria}

Setting the right side of the model system equal to zero and solving for equilibrium yields the following non-trivial equilibrium densities :
\begin{align}
H_1^* &= \frac{\delta_{P2}}{a(f_P)^m} \nonumber \\
H_2^* &= H_1^* * \frac{\theta_{H} (\rho^{\frac{1}{n}}-1)}{\delta_{H2} (\rho-1)} \nonumber \\
P_1^* &= P_2^* * \frac{\alpha_P \delta_{P2}}{\theta_P (f_P)^{m-1}}  \\
P_2^*&= \frac{\theta_H}{a f_H} (\rho^{\frac{1}{n}}f_H -1) \nonumber
\end{align}

With :
\begin{align}
f_* &:= \frac{\theta_*}{\theta_* + \delta_*}, \quad \text{$_*$ equals H,P} \nonumber  \\
s_H^* &:= \frac{\theta_H}{\theta_H + \delta_H + aP_2^*} = \rho^{-\frac{1}{n}} \nonumber \\
\alpha_H^* &:= \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (s_H^*)^i   \\
\alpha_P^* &:= \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} (f_P^*)^i \nonumber
\end{align}

These equilibria are strictly positive if :
\begin{equation}
(\frac{\beta}{\delta_{H2}})^{\frac{1}{n}} f_H  > 1
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Parametrisation}
To find the mean development time, or expected lifespan of juveniles that reach maturity, the Erlang distribution properties can be used.
For clarity, let us reason for hosts in the following equations. Parasitoid terms are set in the same way.  Let us recall that the maturation time is Erlang-distributed with rate $\theta_H$ and shape n. For juveniles, the mean maturation time $\hat{\tau_{H}}$, the variance of maturation time $\sigma^2_{H}$ and the coefficient of variation of maturation time $CV_{H}$ would be : 
\begin{align}
\hat{\tau_{H}} = \frac{n}{\theta_{H}} \nonumber \\
\sigma^2_{H} = \frac{n}{(\theta_{H})^2} \\
CV_{H} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \nonumber 
\end{align}

Setting  $\hat{\tau_{H}} = 1/m_H$, the maturation term becomes :
\begin{equation}
\theta_H = n*m_H
\end{equation}

Also, setting $\rho$ the mean number of offspring yielded by a host individual during its adult lifetime, we get :
\begin{equation}
r = \rho \delta_{H2}
\end{equation}

However, in the previous lines, juvenile intrinsic mortality was not taken in account. Alternatively, a continuous-time stochastic agent-based model can be built. This intuitively accounts for the stochastic process lying behind the model assumptions.

Consider a juvenile agent that either matures or dies. Maturation time $\tau_m$ is drawn from the Erlang distribution $\varphi(\theta_H, n)$ and death time $\tau_d$ is drawn from the Exponential distribution $\mathcal{E}(\delta_H)$. If $\tau_m \leq \tau_d$ then the agent matures. Once adult, new juveniles are produced from a Poisson point process of rate $\beta$.

Thus, let $f_{surv,H1}$ be the mean proportion of surviving host individuals and (13) become :

\begin{align}
\hat{\tau_{H}} = \frac{n}{\theta_{H} + \delta_{H}} \nonumber \\
\sigma^2_{H1} = \frac{n}{(\theta_{H} + \delta_{H})^2} \\
CV_{H} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \\
f_{surv,H1} = (\frac{\theta_{H}}{\theta_{H} + \delta_{H}})^n
\end{align}

Still setting $\hat{\tau_{H}} = 1/m_H$, the maturation and intrinsic mortality terms then become :
\begin{equation}
\begin{alignedat}{2}
\theta_{H} = n * m_{H}* (f_{surv,H1})^{1/n} \\
\delta_{H} = n* m_{H}* (1-(f_{surv,H1})^{1/n})
\end{alignedat}
\end{equation}

We notice that the existence of a positive non-trivial equilibrium can be rewritten as :
$$\rho^{\frac{1}{n}}f_H = (\rho f_{surv,H1})^{\frac{1}{n}} > 1 \qquad \impliedby \qquad \rho f_{surv,H1} > 1$$

\subsubsection{Convergence to ODE and DDE models}
If $n,m = (1,1)$, the Erlang distribution is equal to an exponential distribution and the system is equivalent to its simple ODE system counterpart. Then, $f_*$, $s_H$, $\alpha_*$ are all equal to 1 and equilibrium densities are those found in the simple ODE system. \\ \\

If $n,m\to\infty$, the Erlang distribution of maturation time converges to a dirac distribution and the system is equivalent to its DDE counterpart. Then, equibrium densities become : 
\begin{align}
H_1^* & \xrightarrow[n,m\to\infty]{} \frac{\delta_{P2}}{a\gamma} \nonumber \\
H_2^* & \xrightarrow[n,m\to\infty]{} \frac{ln(\rho)}{\rho-1} \frac{\delta_{P2} m_H}{a \gamma \delta_{H2}} \nonumber \\
P_1^* & \xrightarrow[n,m\to\infty]{} P_2^{* \infty} \frac{\gamma -1}{\gamma} \frac{\delta_{P2}}{m_P ln(\gamma)} = \frac{(\gamma-1)m_H(ln(\rho) - \frac{\delta_{H1}}{m_H})}{\gamma  a  m_P   ln(\gamma)} \\
P_2^*&  \xrightarrow[n,m\to\infty]{} \frac{m_H}{a}(ln(\rho) - \frac{\delta_{H1}}{m_H}) \nonumber  \\
\text{With} \quad \gamma & := e^{-\frac{\delta_{P1}}{m_P}} \sim_{m \to \infty} (f_P)^m \nonumber
\end{align}


Expectedly, they are strictly equivalent to equilibrium densities given in Murdoch 1987.
